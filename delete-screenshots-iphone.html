How to delete screenshots without losing useful information

Find Screenshots in Apple Photos, select the captures you no longer need, and confirm deletion. Review tickets, receipts, reference numbers and other useful information before treating a whole screenshot collection as clutter.

Start in the Screenshots collection

In Apple Photos, find Screenshots within the media-type collections. On iOS 17 these appear in Albums; on iOS 18 they are on the main Photos screen; on iOS 26 start in Collections. Collection placement may vary with your layout. Open Screenshots, use Select to choose only the captures you have reviewed, then use the trash control and confirm. Start with a short visible batch.

Before selecting anything, choose a scope: old shopping research, completed travel plans, or captures from a particular month. That scope is more useful than a blanket rule about age. An old receipt may matter, while yesterday’s throwaway comparison may already be irrelevant. If you have hundreds of captures, do not start by assuming they all have the same purpose. Break the work into recognizable topics.

Separate information from temporary reminders

Screenshots often act as a second inbox. A confirmation page, a useful recipe, a reference number and a funny message may all sit together. For each capture, ask whether you need the picture itself, the information inside it, or neither. If the information still matters, decide where you would naturally look for it later. A travel document belongs with the rest of that trip’s documents.

Choose a way to retain important information before removing its screenshot. For example, save the original document, record the reference in your notes, or keep the capture until the event has passed. Verify that the retained version is readable and complete. Do not substitute a partial number or a cropped preview for the full information you actually need. This is a workflow recommendation, not a claim that every screenshot should be moved elsewhere.

Use a three-pile decision

Give yourself three outcomes: keep, remove and revisit. Keep active tickets, ongoing work references and sentimental captures. Remove material you recognize as finished or unnecessary. Revisit anything whose purpose you cannot remember quickly. The third outcome helps you make progress without converting uncertainty into deletion. You can return to that group after checking your calendar or asking yourself what project it belonged to.

An example: a completed furniture search leaves several nearly matching product captures. Keep the purchase confirmation if it remains useful. Remove rejected options you know you will not revisit. Leave an unfamiliar warranty screen until you have checked whether it belongs to the item you bought. A cleanup session feels more reliable when the reasoning stays connected to real tasks rather than a general “old means bad” rule.

Be deliberate with sensitive captures

A screenshot may contain more private information than the thumbnail reveals. Check captures of accounts, identity documents, conversations and work material carefully. If you retain a sensitive capture, consider where it belongs and who can access that location. If you no longer need it, review the deletion carefully. Avoid uploading screenshots to an unfamiliar web tool just to obtain a cleanup recommendation.

For this task, a readable local review is enough. Enlarge any text that influences your decision and verify whether the whole capture is relevant. A useful detail near the bottom of a long screenshot is easy to miss. Conversely, a capture of a page you can easily reopen may be less useful than an actual document. Make that distinction one item at a time when the consequences matter.

Use a focused screenshot session

Photo Cleaner: Swipe & Delete offers screenshot review as part of its photo-library workflow. Pick that category when you want to work through captures without bouncing between unrelated photos. Keep choices simple and review the selected removals before confirming. If you notice that you are guessing about what an image contains, stop and open it more carefully. A swipe is a convenient decision gesture, not a reason to skip reading.

Try a short session around one completed task. Keeping the context consistent makes decisions easier: all the captures relate to the same trip or purchase rather than years of unrelated reminders. After the session, inspect your remaining references. If the collection now contains only what you can explain, it is easier to maintain. The built-in Photos approach remains a good option when your batch is already small.

Keep the next backlog small

After a booking, delivery or finished project, review its captures while the purpose is fresh. Before taking a new screenshot, ask what you expect it to do: remember a link, preserve a document, or record something temporary. Choosing a destination at capture time can reduce the later work. The point is not to eliminate screenshots; it is to keep them useful and findable.

Use a recurring personal habit such as checking captures after travel rather than aiming for an empty collection. For storage concerns, compare the result with actual device storage instead of promising savings from an image count. If the phone is still full, review large videos next. Before a wider cleanup, read about iCloud deletion so your expectation matches your sync settings.

Questions, answered.

Should I delete all screenshots at once?

Only if you have reviewed them and are sure none are useful. Tickets, receipts, account details and work references deserve a closer look.

Does removing screenshots remove my regular photos?

Selecting captures in a screenshot collection scopes your choices to those items. Still inspect the selection and confirmation before accepting it.

How do I keep screenshots under control?

Review captures when the task that created them ends. Save necessary information somewhere you can find it, and defer unclear items instead of guessing.
