How to delete duplicate photos on iPhone

Open the Duplicates collection in Apple Photos, review a set, and use Merge to keep one combined copy. The route depends on your iOS version, and photos that merely look alike may need a separate manual review.

Start with Apple Photos

Use the built-in duplicate tool before adding another app. On iOS 17, open Photos, tap Albums, and find Duplicates under Utilities. On iOS 18, find Utilities on the main Photos screen, then Duplicates. On iOS 26, tap Collections, then Utilities, then Duplicates. Tap Merge beside a set and confirm the number of items. These routes are documented in Apple’s version-specific guides below.

Test one obvious pair first. Choose a copy you recognize and look at both images at a comfortable size. This small trial lets you understand the result before tackling the rest of the library. There is no need to treat every proposed pair as a decision you must make today. A short first pass is easier to check than a large, rushed batch.

If Duplicates is missing

An absent collection does not tell you how tidy the entire camera roll is. Apple says duplicate discovery first requires indexing and detection. Leave the iPhone locked and connected to power; depending on library size and background activity, detection may take a few days. When Photos has no detected duplicates, the collection does not appear. The Apple troubleshooting link explains these conditions.

While you wait, do something that does not depend on detection: review a familiar trip, old screenshots, or a small collection of repeated downloads. Resist repeatedly adding copies just to test the tool. That creates more clutter and makes the eventual results harder to interpret. Make a note of the originals you want to compare later, then return after the phone has had an uninterrupted charging period.

Check what a copy means to you

A picture can have a purpose beyond its visible subject. You may have an original for future editing, a crop for a profile, and an annotated copy for explaining something to a friend. Before removing a version, ask whether its edits or context are still useful. If you cannot answer confidently, leave that set for a later session. Freeing space is useful only when the remaining collection serves you better.

For an easy decision, start with an accidental repeat download. For a difficult decision, compare the whole frame: text, expressions, edges, and anything added to the image. Avoid deciding from a tiny thumbnail. You are choosing what belongs in your library, not trying to meet a deletion quota. A copy worth keeping can remain even if an app puts it beside another one.

Handle similar shots separately

Two pictures from the same moment may be different photographs. A child’s expression, a moving pet, or a person entering the background can make one frame meaningful. The word similar is a request to compare, not an instruction to delete. Keep both when they tell different parts of the story. For a holiday landscape, you may prefer one broad view and one closer detail rather than a single winner.

An efficient order is to identify your favorite first, then assess the remaining frames against it. If you are preparing an album, think about how the pictures work together. Five versions of the same pose may be unnecessary, while two complementary images may be exactly right. The separate similar versus duplicate guide gives a more detailed decision process.

Use a focused review when grouping is not enough

Photo Cleaner: Swipe & Delete provides a swipe-and-review workflow for photo-library cleanup. Use a small, recognizable category for your first session. Make your keep-or-remove choices deliberately, then inspect the selections before confirming deletion. If a group includes something uncertain, keep it and move on. Review is the useful part of the workflow; a larger selected count is not a better result by itself.

Before starting, decide on one rule such as “keep the best family portrait and every genuinely different expression.” That rule prevents inconsistent decisions halfway through a group. Stop when you no longer feel attentive. A repeatable ten-minute session is often more practical than committing to clean an entire library at once. The app’s role is to organize the work you choose to do.

Finish with a check, not a target

After your pass, open the events you touched and confirm that the pictures you wanted are still there. Check a few important examples rather than relying only on a count. Write down what worked: perhaps repeated downloads were simple, while edited photos needed more attention. Next time, start with the easier category and leave enough time for the difficult decisions.

Before any cleanup, remember that iCloud Photos synchronizes deletions. Do not treat another synced device as an independent backup. Read the iCloud deletion guide if your goal is to free local space while preserving originals elsewhere. Avoid immediately making every deletion permanent; give yourself time to spot a mistaken choice. The goal is a library you can navigate with confidence.

Questions, answered.

Does Apple Photos find every similar picture?

Do not assume that a Duplicates result covers every lookalike frame. Use it for the sets Apple detects, then compare different shots of the same subject separately.

Why can’t I see Duplicates?

Apple requires time to index and detect duplicates while the phone is locked and on power. The collection is absent when no duplicates are detected.

Should I delete every extra photo in a group?

Keep versions with a separate purpose, edit or moment. A useful original and an annotated copy can both belong in your library.
